First, ask the right question

Count?

A binomial model fits counts of successes when n is fixed, trials are independent, each has success/failure outcomes, and p stays the same.

Measure?

A normal model can fit continuous measurements with an approximately symmetric, bell-shaped distribution. Not every measurement is normal.

Look for the chart

Discrete probabilities are bars; normal probabilities are areas under a bell curve. The chart alone does not establish the model.

Fast check

Binomial: X ∼ B(n, p)     Normal: X ∼ N(μ, σ²)

For binomial questions, the variable is discrete and the probabilities add across count values. For normal questions, probability is an area under a curve, not a single point.

Quick guide · Easy understanding

Probability distributions, made easy 🌱

Binomial picture

A binomial variable counts successes in fixed n independent trials, each with two outcomes and the same success probability p.

What the formula says: P(X = k) is the chance of exactly k successes. Count the ways the k successes can sit among the n trials (nCk), then multiply by the chance of one such arrangement: p for each success and (1 − p) for each failure.

P(X = k) = nCk pk(1-p)n-k
P(X=2) = 10 × 0.5² × 0.5³ = 0.3125

Normal picture

A normal model can fit measured values with an approximately symmetric, bell-shaped distribution; not all measurements meet this assumption.

z = (x − μ) / σ
z = (35 − 30)/5 = 1

4.4.1–4.4.2 · Counting successes

4.4.1 Binomial distribution

Use X ∼ B(n, p) to count successes in fixed n independent trials, each with success/failure outcomes and the same p.

4.4.1 Think it through first

Imagine tossing a fair coin three times. Before reading the example below, ask yourself:

  1. How many tosses are there altogether? What is n?
  2. What counts as a success, and what is the chance of success on each toss? What is p?
  3. If X counts heads, what does k = 2 mean?
  4. Are the number of tosses fixed, are the tosses independent, and is the chance of heads the same each time? Why does this make the situation binomial?
  5. What would P(X = 2) tell you in everyday words? Is it a guaranteed result or a chance?

4.4.2 Think about the bars first

Use the three-coin example and its bar chart below. Before reading the explanation, ask yourself:

  1. What count value k does each bar stand for?
  2. For each k, how many of the 8 possible toss sequences have that many heads? How could you turn that count into a probability?
  3. How do you read a bar’s height? What probability does it show for its k value?
  4. Which bars look tallest and shortest? How do their heights compare?
  5. What do all the bars together tell you about the possible numbers of heads and their chances?

Start with three fair coin tosses

Let X count heads. Each of the 8 sequences has probability 1/8. Grouping by X gives 1, 3, 3, 1 sequences.

From sequences to probabilities

P(X = 0) = 1/8,   P(X = 1) = 3/8,   P(X = 2) = 3/8,   P(X = 3) = 1/8

The probabilities sum to 1. Here n=3 and p=0.5; the middle bars are three times the outer bars.

4.4.1 The core count

Two ideas before the formula.

  • Combination ⁿCₖ: the number of ways to choose which k of the n trials are successes. For 3 flips with 2 heads, ³C₂ = 3: HHT, HTH and THH.
  • 0! = 1, so ⁿC₀ = 1 (one way to get no successes) and ⁿCₙ = 1 (one way to get all successes).
P(X = k) = nCk × pk × (1 − p)n − k

4.4.2 Binomial bars

For a binomial model, total probability is 1, and all bars represent count values k = 0, 1, 2, ..., n.

Another example: basketball shots

Suppose a player takes 10 independent shots and makes each one with probability 0.7. Let X be the number of makes, so X ∼ B(10, 0.7).

P(X = 7) = 10C7(0.7)7(0.3)3 ≈ 0.2668

The expected number of makes is E(X) = np = 7, but seven makes are not guaranteed.

Try it: What is the probability of exactly 8 makes?

Check your answer

P(X = 8) = 10C8(0.7)8(0.3)2 ≈ 0.2335.

Real-life example: quality control

A factory checks 20 bulbs. Each bulb is defective with probability 0.05, so X ∼ B(20, 0.05).

P(X = 1) = 20C1(0.05)1(0.95)19 ≈ 0.3774

About 38% of batches of 20 contain exactly one defective bulb.

4.4.3–4.4.5 · Continuous measurements

4.4.3 Normal distribution

A symmetric bell curve centred at the mean μ. The standard deviation σ controls its spread. We write X ∼ N(μ, σ²), then use z-scores and the normal table to find probabilities.

Read the mean and SD first

The mean is the centre; σ measures horizontal spread. For a normal model, approximately 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of values lie within 1σ, 2σ, and 3σ of μ.

Example: test marks with μ=70 and σ=10 have about 68% of marks between 60 and 80. These percentages are rounded empirical-rule values.

4.4.5 Turn a measurement into a z-score

The calculator starts with delivery times: μ=30 minutes, σ=5 minutes. A delivery taking 35 minutes is one SD above the mean.

z = (x − μ) / σ   →   P(X ≤ x) = Φ(z)

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value is above or below the mean.

Different example: the raw/z diagram uses test scores with μ=70 and σ=5, not the delivery-time model or the σ=10 empirical-rule example.

4.4.5 Probability is area

The calculator shades the cumulative area to the left of its cutoff. A right tail uses 1−Φ(z); a between area subtracts two cumulative probabilities.

For a continuous distribution, P(X = x) = 0. Therefore < and ≤ give the same probability. The plotted window shows μ±4σ; the calculated probability includes the full tails.

4.4.4 Read a standard normal table

This lesson uses Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z), the cumulative area to the left. Choose the row for the units and tenths, then the column for the hundredths. For z=1.23, use row 1.2 and column .03. For negative values, use Φ(−a)=1−Φ(a).

Other tables may give the right tail or the area between 0 and z. Check the heading and shaded diagram before using a table value.

Advanced · Combine the pieces

Beyond “exactly one value”

Exact, at most, and at least

Binomial questions can ask for exactly one value or a whole range of values. Add probabilities carefully.

P(X = k) = nCkpk(1 − p)n-k
P(X ≤ a) = Σk=0a P(X = k)
P(X ≥ a) = Σk=an P(X = k)

Normal ranges

Normal probability for a range is the area between two z-scores.

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = Φ(zb) − Φ(za)

Five common mistakes, fixed

  1. Using variance in a z-score: divide by σ, not σ². In N(30, 25), the SD is 5.
  2. Confusing “exactly” with “at most”: P(X ≤ 3) adds 0, 1, 2, 3, not only 3.
  3. Reading the wrong tail: Φ(z) is the left area. Right tail is 1 − Φ(z).
  4. Mixing success and failure: use p for success and 1 − p for failure.
  5. Ignoring dependence: drawing with replacement keeps trials independent, so binomial applies; drawing without replacement does not, unless a special model is justified.

Seven verified formulas

Binomial: count successes

Use a fixed number n of independent trials, two outcomes per trial, and a constant success probability p. The count k is a whole number from 0 to n.

P(X = k) = nCk × pk × (1 − p)n − k
E(X) = np
Var(X) = np(1 − p)

E(X) is the expected count; Var(X) measures spread. The combination counts arrangements of successes, and 0! = 1.

Normal: measure values

Use a stated or justified normal model for continuous measurements. μ is the mean and σ is the standard deviation, with σ > 0.

z = (x − μ) / σ → P(X ≤ x) = Φ(z)
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = Φ(zb) − Φ(za)

Standardize each boundary: za = (a − μ) / σ and zb = (b − μ) / σ. Φ(z) gives the area to the left; the right tail is 1 − Φ(z).

Three binomial analogies

  • Basketball: 10 independent free throws, each with a 70% success chance. The expected count is 7 makes, not a guaranteed result.
  • Quality control: 20 independently checked bulbs with a 5% defect chance each. Exactly one defect has probability 20 × 0.05 × 0.9519 ≈ 0.3774.
  • Coin flips: exactly 3 heads in 5 independent fair flips has probability 10 × 0.53 × 0.52 = 0.3125, or 31.25%. There are 10 matching arrangements.

Three normal analogies

Assuming each measurement follows the stated normal model:

  • Test scores: mean 70, SD 10. Scores from 60 to 80 have z-scores −1 to 1: about 68.27% of the area.
  • Delivery time: mean 30 minutes, SD 5. Delivery within 35 minutes has z = 1: about 84.13%.
  • Height: mean 165 cm, SD 8 cm. Taller than 173 cm has z = 1: about 15.87% in the right tail.

Diagram set: binomial and normal in one glance

1

8 outcomes of three fair coin flips

What to notice
  • Total outcomes = 8, not 16.
  • Every H/T sequence is equally likely.
  • Groups by number of heads: 0, 1, 2, 3.
Quick question: How many sequences have exactly 2 heads?
Feedback: There are 3 sequences: HHT, HTH, THH. So P(X=2)=3/8. This is a binomial distribution with n=3, p=0.5.
2

Probability table and bars

What to notice
  • Each bar represents one value of k.
  • All probabilities add up to 1.
  • The tallest bar shows the most likely value of k.
Quick question: What is P(X=1), and why are the bars for k=1 and k=2 the same height?
Feedback: P(X=1)=3/8 and P(X=2)=3/8, because each case has 3 sequences. In a symmetric distribution with p=0.5, the two middle probabilities are equal.
3

Decision: binomial or normal?

What to notice
  • Binomial: fixed n, two outcomes, same p, independent trials.
  • Normal: continuous measurement with a symmetric, bell-shaped distribution.
  • Not every count is binomial; not every measurement is normal.
Quick question: Is the number of heads in 5 coin flips binomial or normal?
Feedback: Binomial, if the 5 flips are independent with the same p. Normal may suit delivery times, weights or marks if their distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped.
4

10 component icons: one possible result, not the mean

What to notice
  • This picture is one possible result.
  • The expected value is an average, not a certain outcome.
  • 7 made equals E(X), but it is not the only possible result.
Quick question: Is 7 made a certain result?
Feedback: No. 7 made is the most common result with p=0.7, but another possibility such as 6 or 8 can also happen. E(X)=7 is an expectation, not a required result.
5

Binomial bars: exactly, at most, at least

What to notice
  • "Exactly 2" = only k=2.
  • "At most 2" = add k=0, 1, 2.
  • "At least 2" = add k=2, 3.
Quick question: If X is the number of heads in 3 flips, what is P(X≤2)?
Feedback: P(X≤2)=P(0)+P(1)+P(2)=1/8+3/8+3/8=7/8. It is not only P(X=2). For P(X≥2)=P(2)+P(3)=3/8+1/8=1/2.
6

Normal curve: mean, SD bands, empirical rule

What to notice
  • 68% lie within one standard deviation of the mean.
  • 95% lie within two standard deviations.
  • 99.7% lie within three standard deviations.
Quick question: What percentage of marks are between 60 and 80 if μ=70 and σ=10?
Feedback: About 68%. This is the area from μ−σ to μ+σ, that is 70−10 to 70+10. This is the normal empirical rule.
7

Raw values and the z-axis: standardisation

What to notice
  • The raw value and the z-score represent the same point.
  • z is the number of standard deviations from the mean.
  • A z-score makes it easy to compare two different scales.
Quick question: What is z for x=75 if μ=70 and σ=5?
Feedback: z=(75−70)/5=1. This means 75 is one standard deviation above the mean. Standardisation converts the original scale into standard-deviation units.
8

Left, right and between areas on the normal curve

What to notice
  • Left area = P(X≤x).
  • Right area = P(X≥x)=1−Φ(z).
  • Between area = P(a≤X≤b)=Φ(z_b)−Φ(z_a).
Quick question: What is the difference between P(X≤35) and P(25≤X≤35)?
Feedback: P(X≤35) is the whole area left of 35; P(25≤X≤35) is only the area between 25 and 35. Do not mix up the areas under the curve.

What to notice

  • The row is 1.2, the column is .03.
  • For z=1.23, read 0.8907.
